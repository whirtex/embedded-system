\section{Introduction}

Routine hive inspections are important for assessing colony conditions, but they provide intermittent observations and may disturb the colony when the hive is opened repeatedly. Embedded systems and Internet of Things (IoT) technologies offer a way to complement manual inspections through non-invasive, continuous measurements collected inside and outside the hive. Beemon demonstrates an IoT architecture that combines temperature, humidity, weight, audio, and video acquisition with MQTT communication, a ThingsBoard dashboard, and remote storage. Henry et al. likewise developed a wireless sensor network for continuous in-hive temperature, relative-humidity, and acoustic measurements in an apiary environment \cite{tashakkori2021beemon,henry2019precision}.

A more specific challenge is to infer colony activity and possible changes in colony state from measurements that can be collected without opening the hive. Environmental variables alone do not describe all activity at the entrance or all acoustic and vibrational signals produced by the colony. Tu et al. investigated computer vision on a Raspberry Pi to count bees, identify their positions, and measure in-and-out activity, while Uthoff et al. reviewed microphones and accelerometers for studying queen presence and swarming. These studies motivate a multimodal embedded and IoT solution that combines environmental sensing, entrance-focused computer vision, and acoustic or vibration measurements, while requiring validation across colonies and recording conditions \cite{tu2016automatic,uthoff2023acoustic}.

\textit{Related work.} Recent systems have combined environmental sensing and camera-based observation at the hive entrance. Zheng et al. positioned temperature and humidity sensors at the hive center, margin, entrance, and surrounding environment, and used an internal sound sensor and an entrance-facing webcam. Their system applied YOLOv5 and DeepSORT for multi-bee tracking, reaching 83.5\% $\pm$ 0.7 MOTA and 77.3\% $\pm$ 0.2 MOTP at up to 16 frames per second. Long-term observations also associated changes in bee activity with heavy rainfall, center temperatures between 25--37~$^\circ$C, and relative humidity between 48--67\%. Narcia-Macias et al. developed IntelliBeeHive, an entrance-monitoring system based on YOLOv7-tiny. The reported tracking accuracy was 96.28\%, and the pollen model achieved an F1-score of 0.8319. The authors stated that the mite model was trained with placeholder data and that the available mite data were insufficient for a reliable accuracy estimate. Tu et al. provide a complementary low-cost approach based on Raspberry Pi and background subtraction, with reported $R^2$ values of 0.987 for bee counting, 0.953 for in-activity, and 0.888 for out-activity \cite{zheng2024intelligent,narciamacias2024intellibeehive,tu2016automatic}.

Acoustic and wireless sensing add information that is not available in entrance images alone. Uthoff et al. identify acoustic and vibration monitoring as promising for assessing queen presence and swarming, but report limitations involving small datasets, the lack of common evaluation metrics, feature-engineering differences, and limited generalizability. Beemon demonstrates how temperature, humidity, weight, audio, and video can be collected continuously and transmitted to a remote platform, while Henry et al. show the use of a wireless network for in-hive temperature, humidity, and sound measurements. Together, these studies support a multimodal architecture that combines environmental sensors, entrance-focused computer vision, and acoustic or vibration sensing. They also indicate that the proposed system should treat colony-specific validation, data quality, and model limitations explicitly rather than relying on universal thresholds or unvalidated Varroa detection \cite{uthoff2023acoustic,tashakkori2021beemon,henry2019precision}.

\section{Preliminary Project Scope}

The available components support an initial prototype based on DHT11 environmental sensors, an ESP32-CAM positioned at the hive entrance, a controller with wireless communication, and a real-time clock for timestamping. The preliminary collection plan uses one environmental measurement per minute, short acoustic windows when a suitable microphone is available, and scheduled image windows rather than continuous video transmission. This configuration prioritizes non-invasive environmental monitoring, entrance activity, timestamped data transmission, and reproducible validation.

Some capabilities remain dependent on components and data that have not yet been confirmed. In particular, the KY-038 should be treated only as a simple noise indicator; reliable acoustic recording requires a suitable microphone, and vibration monitoring requires an accelerometer. Pollen, Varroa, queen-presence, and swarming analyses should therefore be presented as experimental extensions until representative labeled data and field validation are available.
