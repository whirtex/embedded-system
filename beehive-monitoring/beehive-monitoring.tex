\section{Introduction}

Manual hive inspections remain important for assessing colony conditions, but they provide intermittent observations and can disturb the colony when the hive is opened repeatedly. Embedded systems and Internet of Things (IoT) technologies can complement these inspections through non-invasive measurements collected inside and outside the hive over longer periods. Beemon demonstrates an IoT architecture that combines temperature, humidity, weight, audio, and video acquisition with MQTT communication, a ThingsBoard dashboard, and remote storage. Henry et al. likewise developed a wireless sensor network for continuous in-hive temperature, relative-humidity, and acoustic measurements in an apiary environment \cite{tashakkori2021beemon,henry2019precision}.

A more specific research challenge is to infer colony activity and possible changes in colony state from measurements that can be collected without opening the hive. Environmental measurements provide important context, but they do not capture all activity at the entrance or all acoustic and vibrational signals produced by the colony. Tu et al. investigated computer vision on a Raspberry Pi to count bees, identify their positions, and measure in-and-out activity, while Uthoff et al. reviewed microphones and accelerometers for studying queen presence and swarming. Together, these studies motivate a multimodal embedded and IoT solution that combines environmental sensing, entrance-focused computer vision, and acoustic or vibration measurements, while requiring validation across colonies and recording conditions \cite{tu2016automatic,uthoff2023acoustic}.

\textit{Related work.} Entrance-focused computer vision has been combined with environmental sensing to measure colony activity without opening the hive. Zheng et al. placed temperature and humidity sensors at the hive center, margin, entrance, and surrounding environment and used an internal sound sensor and an entrance-facing webcam. Their YOLOv5--DeepSORT system reached 83.5\% $\pm$ 0.7 MOTA and 77.3\% $\pm$ 0.2 MOTP at up to 16 frames per second, while long-term observations associated activity changes with heavy rainfall, center temperatures between 25--37~$^\circ$C, and relative humidity between 48--67\%. Narcia-Macias et al. reported 96.28\% tracking accuracy and an F1-score of 0.8319 for pollen detection with the low-cost YOLOv7-tiny-based IntelliBeeHive, but identified insufficient and partly placeholder mite data for reliable Varroa evaluation. Tu et al. provide a complementary Raspberry Pi approach based on background subtraction, reporting $R^2$ values of 0.987 for bee counting, 0.953 for in-activity, and 0.888 for out-activity. Taken together, these studies support entrance imaging and lightweight processing, while also showing that performance must be validated under the lighting, camera, and entrance conditions of the target hive \cite{zheng2024intelligent,narciamacias2024intellibeehive,tu2016automatic}.

Acoustic sensing and wireless infrastructure add information and functionality that are not available from entrance images alone. Uthoff et al. identify acoustic and vibration monitoring as promising for assessing queen presence and swarming, but highlight small datasets, the lack of common evaluation metrics, differences in feature engineering, and limited generalizability. Beemon demonstrates the integration of temperature, humidity, weight, audio, and video with MQTT and remote visualization, whereas Henry et al. evaluate a wireless sensor network for in-hive temperature, humidity, and sound measurements in an apiary. These contributions support a multimodal architecture and a remote data pipeline, but they also indicate that the proposed system must document data quality and validate colony-specific indicators instead of relying on universal thresholds or unvalidated Varroa detection \cite{uthoff2023acoustic,tashakkori2021beemon,henry2019precision}.

\section{Preliminary Project Scope}

The available components support an initial prototype based on DHT11 environmental sensors, an ESP32-CAM positioned at the hive entrance, a controller with wireless communication, and a real-time clock for timestamping. The preliminary collection plan uses one environmental measurement per minute, short acoustic windows when a suitable microphone is available, and scheduled image windows rather than continuous video transmission. This configuration prioritizes non-invasive environmental monitoring, entrance activity, timestamped data transmission, and reproducible validation.

Some capabilities remain dependent on components and data that have not yet been confirmed. In particular, the KY-038 should be treated only as a simple noise indicator; reliable acoustic recording requires a suitable microphone, and vibration monitoring requires an accelerometer. Pollen, Varroa, queen-presence, and swarming analyses should therefore be presented as experimental extensions until representative labeled data and field validation are available.
